Build in the Open

Before you build anything, you learn how to think about building it — and how to leave a trail. This is the process and documentation studio that runs underneath every technical module.

The technical tools in this lab will change every year. This won't. Being able to explain what you built, how you built it, what you decided, and why — and to retrace your steps when something breaks — is the habit that transfers to any project, any field, for the rest of your life. It's also exactly what competitions like TSA and real engineering teams reward most.

There's a difference between a project that works and a project you can defend. Anyone can get lucky and stumble into something that runs. What separates a real engineer — and a winning TSA portfolio — is the ability to walk someone through the whole journey: here was the problem, here's what I tried, here's what failed, here's the decision I made and the trade-off I accepted, and here's why. That trail is not busywork you add at the end. It's the work itself, captured as you go.

This studio has two parts. Part 1 teaches the process and the documentation system, and gives you the templates you'll reuse constantly — do it before Module 0. Part 2 is the ongoing practice: exactly what to document at each of the seven technical modules, building toward a portfolio you'd be proud to submit or show an employer.

An honest note on where this comes from

The process below braids together three established frameworks — design thinking, computational thinking, and the engineering design process — plus real documentation practices used at engineering teams. Each piece is sourced at the bottom of this page. The specific braid is our studio's teaching framework, not a single published model. We're upfront about that on purpose: knowing what's established versus what's a useful synthesis is itself part of thinking clearly.

Part 1 · The process & the system

The process — one loop, four moves

Real projects don't march in a straight line, and neither does this. Think of it as a loop you'll travel many times: understand, decompose, build-and-iterate, and — running through all of it — document. Each move borrows from a framework engineers and designers actually use.

1

Understand the need

from Design Thinking — Stanford d.school modes: Empathize & Define

Before you write a line of code, get clear on who this is for and what they actually need. Whose problem are you solving? What would "better" look like to them? Most weak projects fail here — they build a clever thing nobody needed. Write the problem in one or two plain sentences you could say out loud to a stranger.

2

Decompose the problem

from Computational Thinking — Jeannette Wing, 2006

Break the big, scary problem into small pieces you can actually tackle. Look for patterns you've seen before, strip away the details that don't matter (that's abstraction — the part Wing emphasized most), and figure out the steps in order. A problem you can decompose is a problem you can start.

Get the attribution right

You'll often see computational thinking taught as "four pillars" — decomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction, algorithm design. That four-part packaging came later (BBC/CAS education framing); Wing's original 2006 paper foregrounded abstraction rather than a tidy list of four. Both are useful — just don't credit the four pillars to Wing herself. Knowing the difference is exactly the kind of source discipline this studio is about.

3

Build & iterate

from the Engineering Design Process — an iterative loop (e.g., TeachEngineering: Ask · Research · Imagine · Plan · Create · Test · Improve)

Make the smallest version that does something. Test it. See what breaks. Improve it. Repeat. The exact step names vary from source to source (NASA's version differs from TeachEngineering's — that's normal), but the heart is always the same: you loop, you don't sprint to a finish line. Your first version is supposed to be rough. The point is to learn from it.

4

Document throughout — the connective tissue

from real engineering practice — decision logs, design docs, ADRs, retrospectives

This is the move that ties the other three together and the reason this studio exists. As you understand, decompose, build, and iterate, you capture the trail: what you decided, what you tried, what happened, and why. Not at the end — as it happens, while it's fresh and honest. The rest of Part 1 is the toolkit for doing exactly this.

The system — five artifacts

These are the real documents engineers keep. Each one has a job and a moment. You won't use all five every day, but by the capstone you'll have produced all of them — and that collection is your portfolio.

1 · The Decision Log (your running notebook)

Use it: every work session, from day one.

The heartbeat of everything. A dated running journal of what you set out to do, what you tried, what happened, and what you decided next. This is the direct equivalent of the engineering notebook that PLTW and FIRST Robotics teams keep, and TSA's "Plan of Work Log." When something breaks three weeks from now, this is how you retrace your steps.

2 · The README (write it early)

Use it: as soon as your project has a shape — ideally before you build much.

The front door to your project: what it does, who it's for, how to run it, how it works in a few sentences. There's a real practice called README-driven development (Tom Preston-Werner, 2010) — write the README first, so you build the right thing instead of "a perfect implementation of the wrong specification."

3 · The Design Doc (before anything nontrivial)

Use it: before you build something big — your RAG app, your capstone.

A short plan written before coding, covering context, goals and non-goals, your proposed design, and — the most important part — the alternatives you considered and why you didn't pick them. This is a core practice at engineering teams (Google engineers write these as a matter of course). It forces you to think before you type.

4 · Architecture Decision Records (ADRs)

Use it: every time you make a meaningful, hard-to-reverse choice.

A tiny, standardized note for a single decision: which model, which serving engine, which vector store. The format comes from Michael Nygard (2011) and is deliberately small — Title, Status, Context, Decision, Consequences. Ten ADRs tell the story of your whole project in decisions. This is the single most "industry" habit in the studio.

5 · The Retrospective (when you finish, or when it breaks)

Use it: at the end of a project, and after anything goes badly wrong.

An honest look back: what you set out to do, what actually happened, what went well, what went wrong, and what you'd do differently. Engineering teams call the incident version a blameless postmortem (from Google's SRE practice) — the rule is you focus on causes, not blame, assuming everyone did the best they could with what they knew. That honesty is what makes it useful.

The templates — copy these

Keep these in a folder (a shared doc, a git repo, whatever your class uses). Copy a fresh one each time. Plain text on purpose — the thinking matters, not the formatting.

Decision Log entry

# Decision Log — [your name] — [project]

Date: 2026-09-14   Module/Phase: M1 · Run
Goal today: get an instruct model answering locally
Tried: pulled qwen3:8b, ran it; then hit the API from Python
What happened: chat worked; Python script 404'd until I fixed the base URL
Decided / changed: using the /v1/ path exactly as the docs show
Why: that's the OpenAI-compatible endpoint; my URL was missing /v1
Open question / next: why is the 8B slower than expected? check VRAM

Architecture Decision Record (Nygard format)

# ADR-001: Choose base model for the lab

Status: accepted        (proposed | accepted | superseded by ADR-xx)

Context:
Single 16GB GPU per box. Students need an ungated, open-license
model that fits and runs fast enough for live demos.

Decision:
Use Qwen3-8B (Apache 2.0) as the default. Keep gpt-oss-20b as a
fallback for the boxes with more headroom.

Consequences:
+ No license gate; every student can download immediately.
+ Fits comfortably; fast responses.
- Not the strongest reasoning model; revisit with a DeepSeek-R1
  distill later (that'll be ADR-006).

README skeleton

# Project Name
One sentence: what it does and who it's for.

## The problem        why this exists
## How to run it      exact steps someone else could follow
## How it works       the architecture in 3-5 sentences
## Decisions          links to the ADRs behind the big choices
## Known limits       what it can't do yet (honesty scores points)
## Credits & licenses incl. the license of every model you used

Design Doc

# Design Doc: [project]   —   author · date

## Context & scope     what we're building and what surrounds it
## Goals               what success looks like (be specific)
## Non-goals           what we are deliberately NOT doing
## Proposed design     how it will work
## Alternatives        other approaches + why we didn't pick them
## Cross-cutting       security, cost, privacy, model licensing
## Open questions      what we still need to figure out

Retrospective

# Retrospective: [project]

## What we set out to build
## What actually happened
## What went well
## What went wrong   (causes, not blame — everyone did their best)
## Root / contributing causes
## What we'd do differently
## Action items     (concrete, each with an owner)
The git bonus

Once you're using version control, your commit history is documentation too — an automatic, time-stamped audit trail of every change. Write real commit messages ("fix base URL so Python client reaches /v1 endpoint"), not "stuff" or "asdf." Future-you, and anyone reading your project, will thank you.

Part 2 · The ongoing practice

Documentation only works if it's continuous. Here's exactly what to capture at each technical module, so that by the capstone your portfolio has assembled itself. Small and steady beats a panicked write-up at the end — and it's more honest, because you're recording decisions while you actually remember them.

The capstone documentation portfolio

By Module 6 you assemble one package that tells the whole story of your project. This is simultaneously a professional engineering portfolio and the kind of documented-process submission that competitions reward. It contains:

  • The README — the front door.
  • The Design Doc — your plan and the alternatives you weighed.
  • Your ADR set — the decisions, in order, with trade-offs.
  • Selected Decision Log excerpts — the messy, honest middle: what broke and how you got through it.
  • The Retrospective — what you learned.
  • A short demo — the thing actually working.
Why this is your real credential

Remember from the progression: there's no single industry certificate for self-hosting and building on open models. This portfolio is what fills that gap. "Here is a system I designed, built, hosted, and can defend decision-by-decision" is more convincing to a college or an employer than almost any exam badge — and it's the exact muscle competitions like TSA are built to reward.

Prove it — the studio's own checkpoint Before you start Module 0, set up your documentation folder with a copy of each template, and write your first Decision Log entry: the problem you're drawn to and who it's for. You can't document a journey you haven't started — so start the log first, then take the first step.
